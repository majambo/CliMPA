Mafia Island Marine Park (MIMP) Climate Fact Sheet

Supporting climate-smart MPA management in the Western Indian Ocean.

⇩ Download the full PDF

Abstract

Marine Protected Areas in the Western Indian Ocean are on the frontline of climate change. Rising sea temperatures, shifting ocean chemistry, accelerating sea level rise, and increasingly erratic rainfall are fundamentally altering the ecosystems that MPAs are designed to protect and the livelihoods of the communities that depend on them. Effective MPA management increasingly requires climate literacy: the ability to understand, interpret, and act on climate data.

Mafia Island Marine Park occupies a particularly exposed position within the Western Indian Ocean region. Located within the East African Coastal Current system, MIMP's reefs, seagrass meadows, and mangrove forests are subject to the compound pressures of warming, acidification, and sea-level rise, layered on top of longstanding local stressors, including fishing pressure, sedimentation from the Rufiji River delta, and land-based pollution. The communities that depend on MIMP, approximately 66,180 people on Mafia Island alone, have limited adaptive capacity and few formal safety nets to buffer the economic shocks that ecological degradation brings.

Despite this exposure, climate change remains inadequately integrated into MPA management due to a lack of marine climate change indicators across the Western Indian Ocean region. This fact sheet translates available essential climate variables into decision-relevant information for MIMP's managers, organised around eight climate variables, each presented with a clear narrative from historical trends through to future projections under contrasting emission scenarios.

2. Current Climate & Historical Trends

Sea surface temperature

+0.16°C per decade since 1985 (0.6°C total)

Cooler months show an accelerated warming trend, 25% above historical averages, priming habitats for mass mortality during warming periods such as the 2016 and 2020 bleaching events.

Marine heat waves

150+ days/yr since 2015 (few/none in 1980s–90s)

Marine heatwaves have shifted from rare anomalies to a chronic, near-annual phenomenon. The 2024 event set a new record of 150+ days of prolonged thermal stress.

Ocean acidification

pH 8.08 historical baseline, 2000–2018

As atmospheric CO2 accumulates, declining carbonate ion availability threatens the calcification capacity of reef-building corals and other calcifying organisms.

Salinity

33.61 psu historical baseline mean, 2000–2019

Serves as a placeholder for local hydrodynamic conditions, heavily influenced by freshwater pulses from the Rufiji River delta.

Sea level rise

+3.47 mm/yr 1993–2025 (10.7 cm cumulative)

Accelerates shoreline erosion, exacerbates saltwater intrusion into coastal freshwater lenses, and starves landward ecosystems of migration space.

Storms & cyclone exposure

3 record-intensity systems since 2014

Though traditionally outside the primary cyclone belt, systems such as Hellen (2014), Freddy (2023), and Hidaya (2024) made landfall, fracturing branching coral and stripping coastal protection buffers.

Rainfall pattern

Bimodal Masika long rains (Mar–May), Vuli short rains (Oct–Dec)

Peaks in April (244mm) and March (172mm); driest in July (11mm). Air temperatures range from 23.95°C mean in July to 28.04°C mean in February.

3. Summary of Projected Future Climate (to 2100)

Middle-of-the-road (SSP2-4.5) and high-emissions (SSP5-8.5) pathways. This is generated from the best currently available data; it does not replace site-specific impact or adaptation studies.

Sea surface temperature

Mid-century warming of +1.0°C above baseline is effectively locked in under both pathways. By end-century, warming levels off near +1.5°C under SSP2-4.5 versus +3.0°C under SSP5-8.5. Marine heatwave durations are projected to surge to 300+ days annually by end-century.

Ocean acidification

Under SSP2-4.5, pH anomaly reaches -0.09 by 2050 and -0.13 by end-century. Under SSP5-8.5, acidification accelerates sharply to -0.13 by 2050 and -0.27 by 2080–2090, severely impeding coral and coralline algae calcification.

Sea level rise

Likely range of +0.42 to +0.89 m under the low-emission scenario, and +0.61 to +1.17 m under the high-emission scenario, by 2100.

Salinity

Considerable interannual variability, roughly -6 to +2 PSU around baseline. High-emission scenarios suggest a persistent slight negative anomaly of about -2 PSU across decades, driven by intensified freshwater runoff.

Storms & extreme events

A category-dependent pattern: weaker systems (tropical storm through Category 3) show median frequency declines of roughly -10% across the Tanzanian EEZ, while Category 5 landfalls show a median increase of +5.2%, a "fewer but more intense" signal. Confidence is low.

Rainfall & freshwater runoff

Seasonal projections indicate rainfall increasing by a median of +3% to +15% across both pathways, with shifts toward concentrated, intense rainfall events driving high-volume terrestrial runoff.

4. Impacts, Risk & Vulnerability

Risk is understood as the product of hazard exposure, ecological/social sensitivity, and adaptive capacity.

Ecological systems

Coral reefs

Very high

Warming seas and increasingly frequent marine heatwaves are pushing conditions beyond coral thermal tolerance, making annual bleaching events likely to become the norm. Ocean acidification weakens the calcification corals need to recover between stress events.

Exposure: very high · Sensitivity: very high · Adaptive capacity: low

Seagrass meadows

Very high

Rising sea temperatures increase the likelihood of thermal stress episodes, raising the risk of periodic die-off. Recovery depends on water clarity, itself vulnerable to erosion and sedimentation.

Exposure: high · Sensitivity: high · Adaptive capacity: low

Mangrove forests

High

Rising sea levels raise the likelihood of widespread inundation in stands that lack space to migrate landward. Sustained waterlogging converts these areas from carbon sinks into carbon sources.

Exposure: high · Sensitivity: medium · Adaptive capacity: low–medium

Seaweed

Very high

Sustained ocean warming pushes cultivated seaweed beyond its thermal tolerance with increasing frequency. Declining nearshore salinity from increased rainfall and Rufiji River discharge adds osmotic stress.

Exposure: high · Sensitivity: high · Adaptive capacity: low

Socioeconomic sectors

Ecological degradation at MIMP translates directly into livelihood losses for some of the most economically vulnerable communities. Compound losses across reef fisheries, seagrass-associated species, and intertidal foraging will reduce protein availability for a population already dependent on marine resources for nutritional security.

Artisanal fisheries

High

Continued warming and more frequent bleaching are likely to drive a substantial decline in reef fish biomass, reducing catch income, with no formal safety net currently in place.

Seaweed farming

Medium-high

Warming and declining nearshore salinity together raise the risk of crop failure and income volatility, with no climate-resilient cultivar substitution programme in place.

Tourism

Medium

Reef degradation will reduce MIMP's international draw for diving and snorkelling, threatening around 120 enterprises; beach erosion may affect resort infrastructure by 2050 under higher-emission scenarios.

Coastal settlements

High

Accelerated sea-level rise combined with intensified storm surges presents a near-certain hazard for low-lying residential areas: displacement, well contamination, and infrastructure damage.

5. Implications for MPA Management

Climate-smart zoning

Identify and strictly protect climate refugia reef sites with natural thermal buffering; establish adaptive management triggers that activate enhanced protection automatically; protect landward migration corridors for mangroves.

Monitoring priorities

SST loggers; annual GCRMN-protocol coral transect surveys; ecosystem mapping; community-based catch monitoring aligned to TAFIRI stock assessments. All data stored in FAIR-compliant format.

Early-warning systems

A tiered bleaching early-warning system: NOAA CoralWatch DHW alerts for MIMP coordinates; retrieval and analysis of installed data loggers; response protocol activated at DHW 4°C-weeks (watch) and 8°C-weeks (warning). SLR early-warning via tide gauge anomalies and pre-storm surge alerts.

Integration into management plans

MIMP's next General Management Plan revision should include a climate change section, quantified review thresholds, an adaptive management timeline, a climate finance strategy referencing Green Climate Fund eligibility, and an annual data review requirement.
